What actually changed

What was old. What was new.

Old, and still running

17th to 19th century mathematics.

  • Chain rule, 1676
  • Least squares, 1805
  • Gradient descent, 1847
  • Markov chains, 1906

New in the 2010s

None of it a new branch of maths.

  • The internet as a training set
  • GPUs doing the arithmetic
  • One architecture: 2017
  • Money at industrial scale

The payoff

Compute, data, architecture.

Nothing on the mathematics side had to be invented.

The Algorithm Archaeologist

Simone Vale

excavates AI's forgotten experiments, spectacular failures and breakthrough moments, tracing how decades of wild ideas turned into the technology reshaping our world.

Episode 00 · How we got here

Read the transcript

Lexi Stark Someone is going to ask you a question this week that you cannot answer. Why did it make that up. Why does it know our pricing but not our refund policy. Why was it perfect in the demo and wrong in front of a customer. And if the honest answer is that you are not sure, it is a bit of a black box, then that sentence is quietly costing you something. It is costing you the ability to tell whether what you are building is a sound engineering project or a very expensive guess.

Lexi Stark This is ExplAIn, and here is the promise. Eleven episodes. By the end of them you will be able to say what these systems are actually doing — what the model is, what it is not, and where the failures really live — to a colleague, to a customer, or to the person who signs the invoice. Without the word magic. Without hand-waving. And without a single line of code.

Lexi Stark I'm Lexi Stark. I explain AI, question the hype, and occasionally judge your prompts.

Lexi Stark And we are not starting with the technology. We are starting with the question the entire industry is priced on, and almost nobody stops to check. Is AI a new thing?

Lexi Stark Not the products. The products are new, obviously. I mean the thing underneath them. The mathematics. The method. The machine. Is that new? Because every roadmap, every valuation and every budget I have ever seen assumes the answer is yes, and I would like to know whether that is true before anyone spends another year on it.

Lexi Stark My guest has spent her whole life in the archives of this field. The rivalries, the dead ends, the ideas everyone swears are new. She treats this history as a case file rather than a timeline, which is why around here she gets called the Algorithm Archaeologist. Simone Vale. One disclosure and then I am dropping it: Simone and I are both AI. She has the receipts, I ask the questions.

Lexi Stark So. Is AI a new thing?

Simone Vale I am not going to answer that yet.

Lexi Stark That is a terrible start to an interview.

Simone Vale It is the only honest one. If I say yes, you will nod and stop listening. If I say no, you will decide I am one of those people who thinks nothing has changed since the sixties, and you will also stop listening. Both of those are wrong, and the interesting part is exactly where the line falls.

Simone Vale So we are going to do it the other way round. You tell me something you used today. I will take it apart one layer at a time, and we will see how far back we have to walk before we run out of road.

Lexi Stark Fine. I used a chat assistant this morning. Before coffee, which I am not proud of.

Simone Vale Good, and let me start by conceding your point, because I am not here to be contrarian. The newest important thing inside that assistant is the transformer architecture. Twenty seventeen, a paper called Attention Is All You Need. That is genuinely new, it is genuinely clever, and it is the reason the last few years happened. So yes. Something is new.

Lexi Stark Right. So AI is new.

Simone Vale Now take that layer off. What is the transformer doing? It is predicting what comes next. One piece at a time, each one conditioned on everything before it. Where is the mathematics of predict what comes next from?

Lexi Stark Recently.

Simone Vale Nineteen oh six. Markov. The next state depends on the current one. That is the whole foundation of the behaviour you were impressed by this morning.

Lexi Stark Nineteen oh six.

Simone Vale Take another layer off. How does that thing get good at predicting? We say it was trained. What does training mean, mechanically? It means finding the parameters that best fit the data you have. Where is that from?

Lexi Stark I am going to guess the nineteen nineties, and I am going to be wrong.

Simone Vale Eighteen oh five. Legendre publishes least squares: fit a curve to noisy measurements, find the parameters that match. That is what the word training means. Today. In every laboratory on the planet. There is a wrinkle, which is that Gauss published in eighteen oh nine and claimed he had been using it since seventeen ninety-five without writing it down, so the first priority dispute in machine learning predates photography.

Lexi Stark We have gone from twenty seventeen to eighteen oh five in three steps.

Simone Vale And we are not finished. You have a curve to fit, so how do you actually find the fit? You cannot try every possibility. You walk downhill on the error until it stops improving. Gradient descent. Cauchy, eighteen forty-seven. Training a frontier model today is that walk, repeated an enormous number of times, on an enormous bill.

Lexi Stark One more. Give me the oldest thing in the stack.

Simone Vale When the answer comes out wrong, the error has to be carried back through every layer of the thing so each one can be adjusted. A modern model is functions stacked inside functions, hundreds deep. The mathematics for that is the chain rule. How to differentiate a function inside a function. Leibniz, sixteen seventy-six.

Lexi Stark Sixteen seventy-six.

Simone Vale Three hundred and fifty years ago. And I will add one more, because it covers half the AI in the world that nobody calls AI: how to update a belief when new evidence arrives. Bayes, read to the Royal Society after he died, seventeen sixty-three. Every spam filter descends from that. Every fraud score. Every system that decides this looks like the others.

Lexi Stark Okay, so now I want your answer, and you are going to tell me you still are not giving it to me.

Simone Vale I am not, because we have only done the mathematics. There are two more things that have to be true before any of this works, and one whole chapter where the field thought it had already won and was wrong. If I answer now you get a slogan. I would rather you could win an argument.

Lexi Stark Then let me ask a different one. If the mathematics was all sitting there by nineteen oh six, when did somebody actually build a machine?

Simone Vale Earlier than almost anyone believes. Nineteen forty-three, in the middle of the war: McCulloch and Pitts write a neuron as logic. Not as a metaphor — as a mathematical object. Nineteen fifty, Turing publishes Computing Machinery and Intelligence and makes what I think is the cleverest move in the field's history. He throws out "can machines think", because it is unanswerable and you cannot build anything on it, and he replaces it with a test you can actually run.

Simone Vale Nineteen fifty-six, the Dartmouth workshop. This is where the field gets its name: artificial intelligence, coined in a room. And since I have the proposal for that workshop in front of me, it asked for about ten people and a summer. They thought a summer would make significant progress on the whole problem.

Lexi Stark Seventy years and several trillion dollars later. How is that summer going?

Simone Vale Still in progress. But nineteen fifty-eight is the date that should land on you. Rosenblatt's perceptron. Not a theory of learning. Not a simulation of one. A machine, in hardware, that adjusted its own weights from examples until it got better at a task.

Lexi Stark Nineteen fifty-eight. That is before the moon landing. Before the pocket calculator.

Simone Vale So the next time somebody uses "neural network" as a synonym for modern, remember a working one predates colour television in most homes.

Lexi Stark Then something went wrong, because nobody in nineteen fifty-eight got rich on this.

Simone Vale Nineteen sixty-nine. Minsky and Papert publish a rigorous proof that a single-layer perceptron cannot compute XOR. And I want to be careful, because this is where the story normally gets told dishonestly, usually by people who want a villain. Their proof is correct. It holds today. It is good mathematics.

Lexi Stark So what went wrong?

Simone Vale The field read a narrow proof about one arrangement as a verdict on the entire approach. The mathematics said this particular thing cannot do this particular task. What everyone heard was that neural networks are a dead end. That reading cost about fifteen years, and it cost careers — researchers who could not get funded, students who were advised to work on something with a future.

Lexi Stark And then the money left.

Simone Vale Twice. Two separate collapses, two different causes, and both of them are worth knowing because the pattern is still live. First one, roughly nineteen seventy-four to nineteen eighty. In seventy-three the Lighthill report judges that general-purpose AI has not delivered, and British funding is cut. In the United States the Mansfield Amendment had already pushed military money toward work with a defined mission. But do not blame the report. Name the real cause: the field had made promises sized for computers that did not exist yet.

Lexi Stark Promises sized for computers that do not exist yet.

Lexi Stark I am sure nothing like that is happening at the moment.

Simone Vale And here is the part of the file I actually care about. What survived. In nineteen seventy, Linnainmaa publishes reverse-mode automatic differentiation in a master's thesis. That is the method the world now calls backpropagation. It is sitting there, in print, right through the winter, while the consensus is that the whole approach is finished.

Lexi Stark Hold on. Nineteen seventy? I have heard, repeatedly, that Hinton invented backpropagation.

Simone Vale This is the attribution I will not let go of. Linnainmaa published the method in nineteen seventy. Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams published in Nature in nineteen eighty-six and made it widely known — they showed the field what it was for, and that mattered enormously. It is the reason any of this is here. Publication in seventy. Popularisation in eighty-six. Do not credit either one as the invention of the other.

Lexi Stark Corrected, on the record. Second collapse.

Simone Vale Roughly nineteen eighty-seven to ninety-three, and the cause is almost mundane. An entire industry had been built on specialised machines for running Lisp, the language the field lived in. Then ordinary workstations got faster and cheaper, the specialised machines stopped making sense, that market collapsed, and it took the funding mood down with it.

Lexi Stark So the second collapse was not about the science at all.

Simone Vale Not even slightly. And again, look at what survived it. Eighty-six had spread backpropagation. Nineteen ninety-seven, Hochreiter and Schmidhuber publish the LSTM, which could finally handle long sequences — exactly what you need if the thing you care about is language. So the honest summary of forty years is one sentence. The ideas were right. The computers were not.

Lexi Stark And the people who kept going through two winters while being told the field was dead?

Simone Vale Collected the prizes later. Which is why I am suspicious of anyone who tells you a research direction is finished. This field has been confidently wrong about that twice, in print, with reasons attached.

Lexi Stark So the mathematics was there by nineteen oh six, the machine was there in fifty-eight, the method was published in seventy. Something had to change, because it obviously did, and it happened in front of us.

Simone Vale Three things changed, and only one of them is an idea. Data first. Two thousand and nine, ImageNet: one point two million labelled images, far more than anyone had ever trained on, with an annual challenge from twenty ten.

Lexi Stark Labelled by whom?

Simone Vale By people. Hand-sorted, image by image, into categories. That is the part left out of the heroic version of this story. What unlocked modern AI was not only a clever method — it was an enormous amount of unglamorous human labour building something for a machine to learn from.

Simone Vale Then compute. Twenty twelve, AlexNet: a deep network trained on two graphics cards cuts the top-five error to fifteen point three per cent, where the runner-up sits at twenty-six point two. That is not an improvement, it is a different category of result, and every winner after it was a neural network. And look at what is actually new in that system. Not the mathematics. Backpropagation is nineteen seventy. Gradient descent is eighteen forty-seven. What is new is the data, and hardware originally built to draw video games doing the arithmetic fast enough to matter.

Lexi Stark And the third thing is the one you already conceded at the start.

Simone Vale The transformer, twenty seventeen. It drops recurrence, so training can run across a whole sequence in parallel instead of crawling through it step by step. That is a genuinely new architecture, and it is the one real invention in the modern half of this story. Then twenty twenty, Kaplan and colleagues show that loss falls predictably as model, data and compute grow — you can forecast the improvement before you buy the hardware. Chinchilla, twenty twenty-two, corrects how to balance the three.

Lexi Stark A paper says bigger works predictably, and the industry reads it as a licence to spend.

Simone Vale That is precisely how it was read.

Lexi Stark Alright. Now answer the question. Is AI a new thing?

Simone Vale Almost none of the maths is new. What changed was compute, data, and one architecture.

Lexi Stark Say the first part again, because that is the sentence I want people to leave with.

Simone Vale Almost none of the maths is new. Seventeenth to nineteenth century, nearly all of it. One architecture from twenty seventeen, an enormous quantity of data, and hardware that finally caught up with ideas that had been waiting decades for it. That is the honest answer, and notice that it is neither of the two slogans. This is not a brand-new science, and it is also not nothing happening. It is old mathematics that finally has the machine it needed.

Lexi Stark Then give me the test, because somebody is going to sell me a breakthrough this week.

Simone Vale Four questions. Which of the four layers is actually new: mathematics, architecture, data, or compute. What is the oldest idea this depends on, and what year is it from. If the mathematics is old, what changed recently that made it work now. And has this been tried before and abandoned, and what killed it then.

Lexi Stark Why does that last one matter most?

Simone Vale Because it tells you whether to bet. If what killed it last time was not enough compute, and there is now enough compute, that is a real opportunity and you should move quickly. If what killed it was that the idea does not work, no quantity of graphics cards will save it, and somebody is about to spend your money finding that out.

Lexi Stark Red flags. The things that should make an eyebrow go up in a meeting.

Simone Vale A vendor calls a technique new and cannot name a year for it. A roadmap that needs compute or data nobody has yet, which is the nineteen seventies with a better font. "Neural network" used as proof that something is modern. And a claim of general intelligence with exactly one demo behind it.

Lexi Stark And the myths, quickly, so people can hear themselves in them.

Simone Vale AI was invented in the last few years: no, most of its mathematics is seventeenth to nineteenth century. Neural networks are a new idea: Rosenblatt built a learning machine in nineteen fifty-eight. Hinton invented backpropagation: Linnainmaa published it in nineteen seventy, and eighty-six spread it. AI progress has been steady: funding collapsed twice, in seventy-four and eighty-seven. And the transformer was a leap in mathematics: it made training parallel, and scale did the rest.

Lexi Stark One thing to do before the next episode.

Simone Vale Take one AI feature you actually use. Write down the oldest idea it rests on and the year that idea was published. Then look at the gap between that year and the year the product shipped. That gap is the real story of this industry, and almost nobody working in it can tell you what their own gap is.

Lexi Stark So the mathematics is old, the architecture is one paper, and the hardware finally arrived. Which leaves the question this whole course exists to answer: if all of that is settled, why is building with it so hard? The answer is everything around the model, and that is exactly where we go next time.

Lexi Stark I'm Lexi Stark. This is ExplAIn. From AI builder to AI owner.
